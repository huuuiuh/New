The porter registration window had a line of three. All of them F-rank. All of them avoiding each other’s eyes.

“Name.”

“Kang Do-yoon.”

“Age.”

“Twenty-seven.”

The clerk typed, stamped, slid a laminated card across the counter without looking at it. PORTER — CLASS F. Association Registry No. 88-0412. Do-yoon knew the number before he read it.

“You’ll want a party,” the clerk said. “Porters who go in alone don’t come out.”

“I know.”

“Board’s on the wall. Good luck.”

The board was a corkboard. Paper slips, phone numbers, the occasional photo. Ten years ago he had stood in front of it for forty minutes and picked wrong.

He didn’t look at the board. He looked at the bench beside it.

Park Mu-jin sat with his arms crossed and his boots planted like he was daring the floor to move. Forty-five, C-rank, a jaw that had been broken once and set badly. He was reading a folded newspaper and not reading it at all.

Do-yoon’s throat closed for exactly one second. Then he made it open.

“Mister Park.”

Mu-jin looked up. “Do I know you, kid?”

“No, sir. The Association posted that you’re looking for a porter. D-gate, Mapo, tomorrow.”

“I posted that an hour ago.” Mu-jin folded the paper. “You read fast for an F.”

“I carry fast, too.”

The older man looked at him for a long moment—at the card in his hand, the letter on it, the hands that were not shaking.

“Pay is eight percent of the stones. You carry what I tell you and you run when I tell you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Six a.m. Don’t be late.” Mu-jin stood, and he was shorter than Do-yoon remembered. “What did you say your name was?”

“Kang Do-yoon.”

“Mister Park,” Mu-jin repeated, tasting it, and snorted. “Nobody’s called me that in twenty years. Fine. Kid.”
