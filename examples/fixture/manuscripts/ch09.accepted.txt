The first floor had gone too easily, and Do-yoon did not trust easy.

“Two hours,” Mu-jin said, wiping his blade on a rag that had stopped being clean somewhere around the third fog beast. “Two hours for a floor that took Baek’s team a day. Either we’re good or the dungeon is bored.”

“It isn’t bored.”

“Then we’re good. Say it, kid. Let an old man enjoy it.”

Do-yoon didn’t say it. He looked at the stairwell instead, at the gray breathing down the steps like the dungeon had lungs.

Past the second-floor stairs the fog rose to their knees.

*Last time, nobody came out of the second floor for three days.* He had been carrying water on the surface, an F-rank porter counting stretchers. Eleven had gone down. Four had come back up, and one of those had been the wrong color.

“Stay on the left wall,” he said. “The fog thins where the wall sweats.”

Mu-jin gave him a look. “You read that in the Association pamphlet?”

“Something like that.”

“The pamphlet says stay in pairs and don’t breathe deep. It doesn’t say anything about sweating walls.”

“Then the pamphlet is behind.”

Mu-jin snorted, but he took the left wall. “A man who argues with the wall,” he said, “ends up talking to it alone.”

“Is that a proverb?”

“It is now.”

The second floor was a hall of pillars, each one thick as a bus and slick with something that was not water. The fog pooled between them, waist-high in the low spots, and it moved against the air, which was the first thing about the Poison Fog Dungeon that had ever made Do-yoon feel cold.

They found the first mana stone in a dead beast’s ribs twenty steps in. Mu-jin crouched, cut it free, and held it up to the dim: a lump the size of a walnut with a slow green light inside.

“C-grade,” he said. “Maybe B on a good scale.” He tossed it to Do-yoon. “Porter carries.”

“I’m E-rank now.”

“You’re E-rank with a porter’s hands. Carry.”

Do-yoon put the stone in the inner pocket, next to the compass, and did not think about the compass.

They worked forward pillar to pillar. Mu-jin took the front, sword low; Do-yoon took the rear with the cheap issue dagger the Association handed every new E-rank along with the rank card, and he watched the fog for the thing he knew was in it.

*A hunter named Oh had died right there, at that pillar with the crack. The team leader had written “ambush from below” in the report, and everyone had read it and nobody had understood it.*

The fog did not come from below. The beast did.

“Mister Park.”

“I hear it.”

Nothing had made a sound. Mu-jin’s shoulders had simply changed, the way a dog’s do before the doorbell. He stopped between two pillars, and the fog around his boots went still, and then it went the wrong way, pulling inward like a tide finding a drain.

“Back,” Mu-jin said. “Slow. Don’t turn your—”

The floor opened.

Not the stone. The fog. It parted like a curtain and something came up through the gap with too many joints, gray on gray, a body the length of a car and a face that was mostly mouth. Tendrils came off it in a fan. They did not lash so much as unfold, patient, sure of the room.

Do-yoon moved before he decided to. That was the thing about ten years of dying: the body learned things the mind never got credit for.

He went right. Mu-jin went left. The tendrils went for the sound of Do-yoon’s boots, and one of them missed his face by the width of a hand and hit the pillar behind him with a wet crack.

“Its eyes are on the underside!” Mu-jin shouted. “Don’t let it get over you!”

That was wrong, but it was the kind of wrong the whole Association believed in.

*It doesn’t have eyes. It has a stomach that glows when it’s about to swallow. Right side, low, behind the third joint.*

Do-yoon did not say that. There was no version of this room in which an E-rank two weeks past his re-measurement explained a fog beast’s anatomy to a C-rank veteran and lived to hear the questions.

He ran instead.

The beast turned with him, the fan of tendrils swinging, and the light came on under its right flank like a lantern behind cloth—dull orange, ugly, pulsing once.

“There,” Do-yoon breathed, and lunged.

The dagger went in to the hilt and the beast screamed, a high wet whistle that made the fog shiver. The tendrils snapped back toward its body. Not toward Do-yoon. Toward the pain.

Except one.

It came from the blind side, from the left, low and fast and straight at the E-rank who had just stabbed its dinner, and Do-yoon saw it late and knew it, the way he had known so many things a half-second late in his first life.

Mu-jin hit him with a shoulder like a door.

Do-yoon went down hard against the pillar and rolled, and heard the sound, and did not need to look.

Mu-jin was on one knee. The tendril had wrapped his left leg from ankle to calf and let go, and where it had touched, the fabric was already gone, eaten to threads, and the skin beneath it was blistering black.

Black venom was climbing Mu-jin’s left calf.

“Get its—” Mu-jin got up. He got up. He put his weight on the leg and his face went the color of the fog and he stayed up anyway. “Get its light, kid, you were right, hit the light—”

Do-yoon was already moving.

The second stab went deeper than the first. The glow under the beast’s flank flickered, guttered, and went out, and the whole animal convulsed and folded back into the gap it had come from. The fog closed over it like a mouth shutting. The floor was a floor again.

For a while neither of them said anything. Do-yoon’s ears rang. Mu-jin breathed through his teeth.

“Luck,” Mu-jin said finally.

“What?”

“You got lucky. That thing turned and you happened to be where it was soft. I’ve seen it happen. Once.” He wiped his face. “Don’t let it go to your head. Luck runs out faster than mana.”

Do-yoon opened his mouth, closed it, and nodded.

*Let him think that. Let everyone think that, for as long as they can.*

When he stood, something slid out of his inner pocket and hit the stone with a small brass click. The compass. Its glass was cracked—had been cracked when he bought it, at a stall in a market that would not exist in eight years—and its needle turned lazily, pointing at nothing.

Mu-jin glanced at the old compass that had slipped from Do-yoon’s pocket, then forgot it. He was busy with his leg.

The compass needle trembled in the fog.

Do-yoon picked it up fast and put it away. He told himself it was the beast. Vibration. The floor. He was very good, after ten years, at telling himself things.

“Let me see the leg.”

“It’s a leg.”

“Sit down, old man.”

Mu-jin sat, finally, because the pillar was there and his body had decided for him. Do-yoon cut the trouser leg open with the dagger. The black had reached the top of the calf in a spreading net, like ink dropped in milk, and the skin around it was hot and hard.

“Healer at the gate,” Do-yoon said. “There’s always an Association healer at a live gate.”

“There’s always a D-rank kid with a first-aid badge at a live gate.” Mu-jin leaned his head back against the pillar. “It’s fine. It burns. Fog beasts burn. It’ll pass.”

It would not pass. Do-yoon knew what the leg would look like in a week. He had seen it on a stretcher in another life, on a man whose name he had never learned.

*Not this time.*

“We’re leaving,” he said.

“We’ve got one stone.”

“We’ve got one stone and one leg.”

Mu-jin laughed. It hurt him, and he did it anyway. “Fine. Fine. But the second one’s in the beast, you know that. Right where you stuck it. Thing eats stones like candy.”

Do-yoon looked at the gap in the fog, at the place where the floor had opened, and thought about how far down a beast could go.

Then he crouched at the edge and put his arm into the fog up to the shoulder.

“Kid—”

His fingers found something cold and round and slick with the beast’s insides. He pulled it out. Green light, walnut-sized, slow.

“Two,” he said.

Mu-jin stared at him for a long moment. Then he held out his hand, and Do-yoon hauled him up, and the old man put his arm over Do-yoon’s shoulder like it was the most natural thing in the world, and they started back toward the stairs together, the fog closing behind them.

On the stairs, Mu-jin stopped.

Not to rest. Do-yoon could feel it in the arm across his shoulders: the weight didn’t change, the breathing didn’t change. Something else had stopped him.

“If it weren’t for you, son, I’d have died first. I pay my debts.”

“You shoved me out of the way. If anyone—”

“I shoved you because you were standing where I needed to be. Different thing.” Mu-jin started walking again. “You’ll understand when you’re old.”

Do-yoon didn’t answer. He had been old once. He remembered it as mostly regret.

The first floor was as empty as they had left it. The light of the gate showed at the end of the entry hall, blue-white, and the noise of the surface came through it faintly: traffic, a siren, somebody’s music. Seoul, going on.

They came out into a cold afternoon that smelled like exhaust and rain. The Association tent was there; the D-rank kid with the first-aid badge was there, and went white when he saw the leg.

“Two mana stones,” the clerk at the folding table said, not looking up from her tablet. “Party of two. Injuries?”

“One,” Do-yoon said.

“None,” Mu-jin said, at the same time, and sat down on a folding chair and let them cut the trousers the rest of the way.

The clerk looked up then. She had the face of someone who had processed four hundred parties and had opinions about all of them.

“Which is it?”

“One,” Do-yoon said. “Left leg. Fog-beast venom, second floor, about forty minutes ago. It’s spreading.”

“Fog beasts don’t—”

“It’s spreading.”

She looked at the leg. The D-rank kid had gotten the trouser off and was holding a bottle of antiseptic like it might apologize on his behalf. The black net had reached the top of the calf and was thinking about the knee.

The clerk put her tablet down.

“Healer’s at the Mapo gate,” she said. “Transport’s twenty minutes if the roads are kind. Sir, I’m going to need you on the stretcher.”

“I walked out of the dungeon,” Mu-jin said. “I can walk to a van.”

“Sir.”

“Kid, tell her.”

Do-yoon looked at Mu-jin—at the gray in his beard, at the hand pressed flat on his own knee to stop it from shaking, at the sweat that had nothing to do with the temperature. He thought about a stretcher in another life, and a sheet, and a name he had never learned.

“Get on the stretcher, Mister Park.”

Mu-jin stared at him. Then he laughed, short and surprised, and it hurt him again.

“Two weeks,” he said. “Two weeks a hunter and he’s giving orders.” But he let the kid help him onto the canvas, and he let them strap the leg, and when the van door slid shut on him he was still muttering about it.

Do-yoon stood in the rain and watched the van pull into traffic.

*Forty minutes.*

Last time, he had learned the number from a report, years after the fact: forty minutes from bite to the point where the venom stopped being a leg problem and started being a heart problem. Nobody had known that then. Somebody would work it out in three years, after enough people died in vans.

Mu-jin had thirty left. Maybe.

“You’re the E-rank,” the clerk said behind him. “Kang. From the measurement hall.”

He turned. She was holding out the two mana stones in a clear bag, tagged and sealed, and looking at him the way people had started looking at him lately—half a second too long.

“You said the second floor.”

“Yes.”

“In two hours. Party of two.” She didn’t make it a question. “Baek’s team spent a day on that floor and came back with one stone and a concussion.”

“We got lucky.”

“That’s what Park said.” She handed him the bag. “Payout’s at the Association office, Monday. Bring your card.”

Do-yoon put the bag in his inner pocket. His fingers brushed the compass on the way, and he took his hand out quickly, and did not check whether the needle had moved.

The rain came down harder. Across the road a screen the size of a building cycled through the evening headlines—a chairman shaking hands, a guild announcement, a gate advisory—and Do-yoon watched the chairman’s face go by, smiling, generous, entirely at ease, and felt the old cold settle in his stomach where it lived.

*Not yet. Not for a long time yet.*

He started walking toward the subway. Somewhere behind him a phone rang in the Association tent, and the clerk answered it, and her voice changed.

Outside the gate, the black venom had climbed to Mu-jin’s knee.
